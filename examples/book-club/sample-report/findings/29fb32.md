## What the rule says to do, in full

This is a candidate for review against GDPR Art. 8, not a determination
about this code. Art. 8
addresses the controller (Art. 4(7)) and is engaged only where two things
hold together: an
information society service is offered directly to a child, and consent
under Art. 6(1)(a) is the
basis for the processing. Settle those two before changing anything - for a
product with an adult
or mixed audience, or for processing that runs on contract rather than
consent, there is nothing
here to fix, and the right outcome is a recorded note of why.
Where both do hold, the engineering questions are these.
1. Which age applies. Art. 8(1) sets 16 as the default and its second
   subparagraph lets each
   Member State legislate a lower age down to a floor of 13, so the binding
   threshold sits
   somewhere in 13 to 16 and is fixed by the national implementing law of
   each Member State whose
   users the service reaches - a service available across the Union faces
   several at once. The
   age of every Member State is carried in this rule: the working list is
   in the context question
   about thresholds, and each row is sourced in legal_basis with its
   national provision, the
   in-force date of the instrument that set it, and whether that state
   derogated from 16 at all.
   A single hard-coded number is the shape to look at hardest: 18 is not a
   GDPR figure and usually
   arrives from a COPPA-shaped or adult-content design, and 13 is the floor
   of the range rather
   than a safe default. Making the threshold a per-country value, sourced
   from the national law
   and defaulting to 16 where the country is unknown, is the change that
   removes the assumption.
   A per-country table is not the end of the work, because the numbers
   move: Denmark went from 13
   to 15 on 2024-01-01 and Slovenia from 16 to 15 on 2023-01-26, so a table
   written before those
   dates is now below the national floor in two states. Whatever holds the
   numbers - a constant,
   a config file, a remote table - needs a date on it and a review that
   outlives the developer
   who wrote it.
2. Whether the age is used. A date of birth that is stored and never read
   by a gating branch buys
   nothing and costs a data-minimisation question under Art. 5(1)(c).
   Either connect it to a
   decision or stop collecting it.
3. What happens below the threshold. Art. 8(1) permits the processing where
   consent is given or
   authorised by the holder of parental responsibility, and Art. 8(2)
   requires the controller to
   make reasonable efforts to verify that authorisation, taking available
   technology into account.
   An unverified self-declaration is the weak point: a parent email that is
   never confirmed
   establishes nothing. Holding the account in a state that collects no
   consent-based data until
   authorisation is confirmed is what makes the verification step
   load-bearing rather than
   decorative.
4. Every entry point, not one. Social sign-in, invitations, API and admin
   account creation, and
   importers routinely bypass the form that carries the age step.
Two adjacent obligations are commonly confused with this one and are not
covered by it: whether
the interface and privacy information are in language a child can
understand (Arts. 12-14 and
Recital 58), and whether the shipped defaults are the most protective ones
for a child's account
(Art. 25). Both are separate rows with their own rules.
